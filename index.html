Portfolio Artifact 02 · Assessment 2.3 · ML vs. Deep Learning

Matching the Tool to the Problem: Machine Learning vs. Deep Learning

A case study comparing two real-world AI applications to show when traditional machine learning is the right choice, when deep learning earns its cost — and why picking the most advanced tool is not always the right business decision.

Objective
Evaluate two real-world AI applications to determine when traditional ML versus deep learning is the appropriate approach, and communicate that decision framework to a business audience.
Process
Selected two contrasting applications — customer churn prediction and CNN-based facial recognition — and analyzed each against three criteria: feature extraction, data requirements, and model complexity. For each, argued both directions: why the chosen approach fits, and why the alternative fails.
Tools & Concepts
Support vector machines, logistic regression, convolutional neural networks; evaluation framing drawn from my analytics work in Python, SQL, and Power BI.
Unique Value
The case study argues both directions for each application — not only why the chosen technique fits, but why the alternative concretely fails (a deep network overfitting a few dozen clean tabular columns; an SVM with no mechanism to discover spatial patterns in raw pixels). That two-sided reasoning is the difference between describing techniques and being able to defend a model selection to stakeholders.
Relevance
Model selection under constraints — interpretability, data availability, compute cost — is the first judgment call in any AI adoption decision, including the AI-assisted ITSM tooling in my own field. This framework is directly reusable when evaluating whether a vendor's "AI-powered" solution matches the shape of the problem it claims to solve.
References
AIML-500 Assessment 2.3 coursework; A Beginner's Guide to Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning (course text).
Traditional ML — Customer Churn

Structured tabular data, known features, modest datasets, and a business need for interpretable predictions.

Deep Learning — Image Recognition

Raw, high-dimensional pixel data where features can't be hand-defined and massive datasets justify network complexity.

Machine learning and deep learning both enable computers to learn from data, but they differ fundamentally in how they handle features, how much data they require, and how complex their models are. A simple way to frame the difference: machine learning is like giving a student a checklist of things to look for, while deep learning is like teaching the student to read and letting them figure out the patterns through experience. This case study compares one real-world application of each approach and explains why the chosen technique fits the problem while the alternative does not.

Machine Learning: Customer Churn Prediction

Telecommunications and subscription-based companies use models such as support vector machines and logistic regression to predict which customers are likely to cancel. A provider can analyze historical records — contract length, monthly charges, tenure, support tickets filed — to flag at-risk customers before they leave, allowing retention teams to intervene with targeted offers.

Traditional ML fits this problem for three reasons. First, the data is structured and tabular, and the meaningful features are already known to domain experts, so manual feature selection is straightforward and effective. Second, churn datasets are often modest in size — enough for an SVM or logistic regression to perform well. Third, the business needs interpretability: a retention team must understand why a customer was flagged, and simpler models provide that transparency directly.

Deep learning is not suitable here. Its core advantage — automatically discovering features from raw, high-dimensional data — is wasted on a few dozen clean database columns. A deep neural network would risk overfitting the limited data, demand far more computational power, and produce a black-box model that is harder to explain to stakeholders, typically with no accuracy improvement over traditional methods on tabular data.

Deep Learning: Image Recognition with CNNs

Facial recognition systems such as Apple's Face ID use convolutional neural networks to identify users from raw camera data. The CNN processes millions of pixel values and learns, through its many layers, to detect progressively more abstract patterns — from simple edges to textures to complete facial structures — without any human defining what an eye or nose looks like.

Deep learning fits this problem precisely because the input is raw and unstructured. No human can manually write features that reliably describe a face across every lighting condition, angle, and individual. The automatic, layered feature learning of a deep network is exactly what the task demands, and today's enormous image datasets provide the training volume that deep learning requires.

Traditional ML is not suitable for this task. An SVM or decision tree fed raw pixels has no mechanism for discovering spatial patterns on its own, so it would depend entirely on hand-engineered image features — an approach that historically capped accuracy well below human performance. The complexity of a deep network is justified here because the problem itself is complex.

Conclusion

When features are known, data is limited, and interpretability matters, traditional machine learning is the better tool. When the data is raw and high-dimensional, features cannot be defined by hand, and large datasets are available, deep learning's added complexity earns its cost. Choosing between them is less about which is more advanced and more about matching the tool to the shape of the problem.

For this coursework activity I worked through a structured session with an AI coaching bot covering the three training paradigms — supervised, unsupervised, and reinforcement learning — along with the role of algorithms, the training workflow, why models need repeated passes over data, and why data quality determines the ceiling on performance. Rather than stopping at the seven assigned questions, I brought my own examples from IT service management, corrected one of the tutor's analogies, answered a question it had left open earlier in the session, and challenged it directly on why I should trust explanations it had supplied without independent verification. Process

Asked where a limited budget should go, I argued for better features over more data, since additional data at small scale tends to add noise and feature work requires no labelling team. Finally I asked the tutor how I could trust information it had entirely supplied, and recorded its answer: cross-reference, test it, ask why. I used that exchange as the closing section of the guide.

Diagnosing Data Failures in Production Machine Learning

Three escalating scenarios — missing data, extreme class imbalance, and a production feedback loop. In every one, the reported metric looked acceptable while the model was failing. This artifact documents how I found the real problem underneath each.

Description

For this coursework activity I completed an interactive session with an AI coaching bot that presented three real-world data challenges of increasing difficulty. Each required a full approach — decisions, reasoning, and solution — before the coach responded with critique and follow-up pressure. For this portfolio I have restructured the conversation into a technical decision brief, presenting each scenario the way a practitioner would document it, and resolving one edge case I left unanswered during the live session.

Scenario 1 — Missing Data in a House Price Model

10,000 property records. Roughly 15% of rows have missing values across year built, square footage, and the target variable sale price.

My approach
  • Target variable: Rows missing sale price cannot train the model, so those rows are dropped — the rows, not the column. But first, check why they are missing. If unsold or withdrawn listings are systematically the ones without a price, dropping them silently narrows what the model is actually trained on.
  • Square footage: Likely the strongest predictor, so the column stays. Instead of a global median, impute from comparable properties — same neighbourhood, same bedroom count — which preserves the relationships between features rather than flattening every missing house to "average." Add a binary flag marking imputed values, in case the missingness itself carries signal.
  • Year built: Lower predictive weight, and probably missing where county records are incomplete. Median imputation with a flag is defensible — unless the missing records skew toward older properties, in which case imputation papers over a real pattern.
  • Leakage control: Compute all imputation statistics from the training split only. Deriving fill values from the full dataset leaks test information into training and inflates evaluation scores.
Where I pushed back

The coach initially framed column usability as a percentage threshold — under 5% safe, over 30% drop. I challenged that: 40% missing completely at random is easier to handle than 10% missing for a systematic reason. The coach agreed and introduced the proper framework — MCAR, MAR, and MNAR. The percentage tells you how much information you lost; the mechanism tells you whether you introduced bias. Only the second one can poison a model.

Edge case I resolved afterward

The coach asked what happens when a neighbourhood has too few complete records to support a reliable group median — a question I did not answer during the session. The answer is hierarchical fallback: impute from the narrowest group that meets a minimum sample size, widen the grouping (neighbourhood → district → city) when it does not, and record which level was used so the added uncertainty stays visible downstream.

Scenario 2 — Extreme Class Imbalance in Fraud Detection

1 million transactions, 0.5% fraudulent. The model reports 99.5% accuracy but catches only 12% of actual fraud.

Diagnosis

The model has learned that predicting "not fraud" is correct 99.5% of the time. Accuracy is not measuring fraud detection here — it is measuring class prevalence.

My approach

My first question was whether classification is the right framing at all. At 0.5% positives, anomaly detection — modelling normal behaviour and flagging deviation — may fit better, particularly because fraud is adversarial and its patterns shift over time. Within a classification framework, the two levers are class weighting, which changes the decision boundary the model learns, and threshold tuning, which changes only how the outputs are interpreted afterward. These operate at different stages; they are not the same adjustment made twice.

The failure I surfaced

Heavy class weighting distorts probability calibration. A weighted model's output of 0.7 no longer means a 70% likelihood of fraud. If downstream systems simply threshold that score, the relative ordering still holds and nothing breaks. But if they consume it as a calibrated risk figure — for tiered verification, cost-benefit logic, or ranking across models — every decision built on it is systematically wrong. The remedy is to calibrate after training and before those numbers are exposed, using Platt scaling or isotonic regression. This failure is invisible in accuracy and in recall.

Scenario 3 — Drift and Feedback Loops in Dynamic Pricing

A ride-share surge pricing model, stable for three months, shows a 40% error increase concentrated in weekend evenings in specific neighbourhoods. It underprices surge there; drivers avoid the zones; riders wait. A competitor launched in those neighbourhoods two weeks earlier and shifted rider price sensitivity.

Diagnosis

This is not only competitor-driven drift. It is a feedback loop. Recent demand data from those zones was generated under the model's own mispricing, so retraining on it means learning from the consequences of the original error. The observed demand is not the demand that would exist at correct pricing.

My approach

Observation cannot resolve this, because every available data point is contaminated by the pricing decisions that caused the problem. The fix is intervention — randomised pricing experiments, or a permanent exploration component in the pricing policy, so the model receives causal signal about the price–demand relationship instead of an echo of its own behaviour. Unaffected neighbourhoods serve as controls, separating competitor impact from self-inflicted damage. In production this is typically implemented through bandit approaches that balance exploration against exploitation, accepting deliberate short-term pricing losses as the cost of not drifting into a worse failure.

What I Took From It

The technical fix was the easy part in all three cases. The harder question was whether I was measuring the right thing to begin with. Accuracy on the fraud model, aggregate error on the pricing model, and a clean-looking dataset in the housing scenario were each concealing the actual failure. That habit — interrogating the metric before trusting it — is what this artifact is meant to demonstrate.

AI-Assisted Ticket Management
